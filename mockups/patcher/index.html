Where does a cable get made?

Four ways to patch one ClackOS app into another, drawn before any of the routing exists. They all obey the same rule and carry the same four port kinds — what they disagree about is where you go to make a connection, and what that costs you in windows, clicks and screen. Drag between jacks, pull cables out, drag the boxes around.

The rule every study obeys

This is the part that is not up for debate in these studies. A port is normalled: it has a default connection that a cable interrupts, exactly like a jack on a Eurorack panel or an old telephone patch panel.

  1. No cable — nothing changes. Sound Recorder asks for the microphone the way it does today. ClackScope's Input dropdown works the way it does today. No patch UI is involved, and none needs to be open.
  2. Cable in — it overrides the normal, immediately. No configuration at either end, no patch entry appearing in the app's own source dropdown. The app shows the override rather than asking about it: the picker greys out and a lamp lights.
  3. Cable out — the normal comes back. The app returns to whatever its own picker last said. Closing a window drops its cables, which is what keeps this free of dangling state.

A cable is a handshake, not a pipe. The desktop introduces two apps and gets out of the data path; what actually crosses depends on the kind, and no patch UI ever touches a sample:

  • bytes → MessagePort
  • audio → MediaStream
  • midi → MessagePort
  • video → OffscreenCanvas
A

Link Menu

No patcher at all. Every app that declares ports grows a Link menu listing the compatible ports on every other open window. This is the one ClackTerm already has — the MIDIterm bridge entries at serial-console.html:236 are this design with the list hardcoded to one app.

Open Link on ClackScope and choose a source. Watch the Input dropdown below it.

Why it works

It is the most automatic of the four. One menu, one click, no window to open, nothing to arrange. The connection is made from the end that wants it, which is also the end you already have your mouse in.

It costs almost nothing to build: the desktop supplies the list of compatible ports, app-bar.js already builds menus, and the app draws no patch UI whatsoever.

Where it hurts

There is no picture. With six windows patched you cannot see the routing without opening six menus, and a cable you have forgotten about is invisible until something sounds wrong.

It also only reads in one direction — you patch into the app you are in. Sending an output somewhere means going to the far app and pulling, which is backwards from how a patch cable feels.

Where the brief lands

  • Normalleda ✓ against the current source, "(normal)" against the default
  • Kindsthe menu only ever lists compatible ports
  • Overridepicker disables, lamp in the status row lights
  • Unpatchpick "(normal)" again
  • Overviewnone — this is the whole problem

No window · ~120 lines in app-bar.js · works today

B

Patchbay

A window of its own, dressed as a rack. Each open app becomes a module with jacks down its face; you drag a lead from one to another and it hangs there. The site already has a Eurorack Panel Generator and a EuroClack section — this is the ClackOS-native answer.

Drag from any jack to another. Click a cable to pull it out.

Why it works

It has the most character by a distance, and character is most of what this site is for. Patching is a physical act — grab, stretch, plug — rather than a menu selection, and the result is legible from across the room.

Two cables into one inlet is visibly a mess, which is exactly the arbitration model I wanted to avoid writing in code. The panel says "you did that" better than a dialog can.

Where it hurts

Modules are a fixed width and apps have wildly different port counts, so a rack of eight windows is wider than the desktop and needs scrolling — at which point the at-a-glance benefit starts to leak away.

It is also the most drawing for the least information: a lot of pixels spent on rails, screws and plate texture that carry no signal.

Where the brief lands

  • Normalledunpatched inlets print their default under the name
  • Kindsjack ring colour; incompatible jacks fade on drag
  • Overrideplugged jack fills, module meter goes live
  • Unpatchclick the lead
  • Overviewcomplete, until it needs scrolling

C

Signal Flow

The Pure Data idiom, which is already in the repo: boxes with inlets on top and outlets on the bottom, arranged however you like. It cares about the graph rather than about the furniture, and it is the only study that stays readable at twelve connections.

Drag a box to move it. Drag from an outlet (bottom) to an inlet (top). Click a link to cut it.

Why it works

It scales. Boxes move, so the user untangles their own patch; long chains read left to right or top to bottom without crossing. Nothing else here survives a genuinely complicated routing.

It is also the cheapest to draw — no plate texture, no rails, no window chrome — and the easiest to make keyboard-navigable, which matters for a site that cares about that.

Where it hurts

It looks like a tool, not like ClackOS. Dropped into a desktop built of cream paper and engraved nameplates, a node graph reads as something imported from a different program.

Box positions are state, and state wants saving — the first thing anyone will ask is why their layout reset, which drags persistence into a design that was meant to avoid it.

Where the brief lands

  • Normalledghosted default printed beside an empty inlet
  • Kindsdot ring colour; bad targets dim on drag
  • Overridedot fills, title lamp lights
  • Unpatchclick the link
  • Overviewthe best of the four, and the only one that scales

D

Desktop Cables

No patcher window either — the desktop itself is the patchbay. Each app window grows a jack in its title bar, and leads hang between windows across the wallpaper. Drag a window and its cables follow it.

Drag a title bar to move a window. Drag from the jack on one title bar to another. Click a cable to pull it.

Why it works

Nothing is indirect. The thing you patch is the actual window, in the actual place you left it, and the routing is drawn over your real desktop rather than over a diagram of it. No second representation to keep in step.

It costs no window and no menu entry, and it makes the desktop feel like a machine — which is the single most on-brand outcome available here.

Where it hurts

One jack per window is not enough. ClackTerm has two ports and Pure Data has three; a title bar cannot hold them without becoming a control panel, so this study quietly assumes a single default port per app and that assumption will break.

Overlapping windows hide their own cables, minimised windows have nowhere to hang one from, and a busy desktop turns into spaghetti drawn over the work you are trying to read.

Where the brief lands

  • Normallednowhere to print it — needs a tooltip
  • Kindsjack ring colour only
  • Overridejack fills; the app shows its own lamp
  • Unpatchclick the cable
  • Overviewgood at three windows, unreadable at eight

Recommendation

Ship A. Add B when there is something worth looking at.

A is the only study that matches what you actually asked for — patching should be automatic, and nothing should stand between you and a connection. It needs no window, no menu entry, no change to clackos.js window chrome and no new persisted state, and the pattern is already in the tree at serial-console.html:224. If exactly one of these gets built, it is this one, and it is the smallest of the four by a long way.

A's single real weakness is that it has no picture. That is an argument for building B later, not instead: the patchbay is a read-only view over the same registry plus drag-to-connect, so it costs nothing but its own drawing and can arrive whenever the routing gets complicated enough to want it. Nothing in A has to change when it does.

C is the right answer to a question this site is not asking. It wins the moment there are a dozen connections, and there will never be a dozen connections — six apps with two ports each is the realistic ceiling, which is comfortably inside what B can draw. Its box positions also drag persistence into a design whose main virtue is not having any.

D is the most fun and the one I would not build. One jack per title bar cannot represent ClackTerm's two ports or Pure Data's three, so it needs a disclosure control in the window chrome to be honest — and at that point it is B, drawn over the wallpaper, in the one place where windows can cover it up. It is worth stealing one idea from: when a window is patched, the title bar should say so.

If this is the direction, next steps

  1. Ports first, UI second. assets/js/app-patch.js in the shape of app-state.js — opt-in, no-op when the page is opened standalone, and handling both the iframe and shadow-root cases the way app-state.js:34-46 already does.
  2. One registry in the desktop. Window id → declared ports, hung off the existing windowIdFor at clackos.js:2206, with a clackos-patch-* message family alongside the ones in handleAppMessage. The desktop mints the MessageChannel and then stays out of the data path.
  3. Normalled means the app decides. The port layer hands over an object and raises an event; overriding the source is the app's own code, so ClackScope's selectSource() at scope.html:584 gains a cable branch and its #src-sel dropdown gains nothing at all.
  4. Two apps, one kind, to start. ClackTerm's serial RX into ClackScope is the smallest useful pair and needs only bytes. Audio can wait until the MediaStream-across-frames question has been tested in Firefox.
  5. Retire the bridge into it. clackos-midi-bridge becomes two ordinary midi ports, and the BroadcastChannel in both apps goes away — the first real test that the abstraction is worth having.
